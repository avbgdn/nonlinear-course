8. Macrokinetics of reactions in the solid state

8.1. Low-temperature explosion

The combustion processes considered in the previous chapter have been studied most thoroughly for high-temperature, predominantly gas-phase, reactions. Even in the combustion of solid fuels, the combustion process often begins with the release of volatile components from the fuel. For example, in the combustion of a paraffin candle, at the first stage the melting of the candle material, the rise of the melted paraffin along the wick and its evaporation take place. The flame of the candle proper is the combustion of gaseous paraffin and of the products of its pyrolysis and partial oxidation. Other solid fuels, for example wood, are unable to melt. However, upon heating of wood, a whole series of volatile substances is formed, such as formic and acetic acids, methanol, methyl acetate and others. The oxidation of these organic substances, which constitute the so-called "wood gas", ensures the combustion process at the initial stage. Later, if the combustion temperature reaches sufficiently high values, the combustion of charcoal – that is, of the carbon proper formed at the initial pyrolysis of wood – may proceed.

The combustion of many solid fuels, for example gunpowder, is also connected with the release of volatile components from the heated material and their subsequent reactions in the gas phase. With this is connected the decrease of the burning velocity of gunpowder at reduced pressure.

On the other hand, a whole class of combustion processes is known in which the reaction takes place between solid components. For example, if a finely ground mixture of titanium and carbon is pressed into a tablet and locally heated, the reaction of formation of titanium carbide begins on the heated surface. This reaction is strongly exothermic, and the heat released turns out to be sufficient to initiate the reaction in a neighbouring part of the sample. Thus, a wave of a high-temperature reaction passes through the sample. Such a process (it is called self-propagating high-temperature synthesis) makes it possible to obtain many refractory substances, such as carbides, borides and silicides of metals.

In fact, a high temperature is also not a necessary condition for the explosive course of a chemical process. This can be seen from the following example. Thus, a paradoxical situation arises: upon a decrease of the temperature, not a decrease but a sharp increase of the rate of the reaction takes place.

The explanation of the observed phenomenon is connected with the structural peculiarities of the solid substance in which the reactions proceed. The mixture of reactants is a glassy material, in fact a strongly supercooled liquid. The molecules in such a medium, compared with molecules in an ordered crystal, are characterised by an excess free energy connected with defects of the glass structure. These structural defects give rise to mechanical stresses in the material. Such mechanical stresses are formed during the preparation of the sample, relax slowly at a given temperature, and are additionally formed during the course of chemical reactions.

The presence of the excess free energy connected with internal stresses of the glass structure leads to the fact that for molecules frozen in a region of high stress of the material, the activation energy of the chemical reaction is lowered. On the other hand, at lower temperatures the relaxation of internal stresses is retarded, as a consequence of which they accumulate more efficiently. 8.2. Kinetic inequivalence in reactions in solids

The consideration of the phenomena of low-temperature explosion brings us to several general conclusions concerning the course of chemical reactions in solid media:

1. The kinetics of chemical processes in solids is closely connected with the structural peculiarities of the medium.

2. Structural relaxation often proceeds with characteristic times comparable to or even greater than the characteristic times of chemical reactions. In this case, relaxation processes must be taken explicitly into account in kinetic models.

3. Under conditions when the structure of the molecular environment influences the chemical reactivity, chemically identical molecules located in matrix cells differing in structure may enter the reaction with rates differing manyfold. This situation is called kinetic inequivalence of molecules in a solid or the dispersion of molecular reactivity.

8.4. Photoselection

8.5. Photoorientation

Another mechanism for obtaining anisotropic samples under the action of light bears the name of the photoorientation process. This process occurs if the concentration of light-absorbing molecules does not change, but the molecules that have absorbed a photon change their orientation in space. The light-stimulated rotational mobility leads to the fact that molecules with a transition dipole moment close in direction to the direction of the electric vector of the light undergo rotational motions, and their number decreases, whereas the number of molecules with a dipole moment directed perpendicular to the electric component of the light wave increases. Thus, under the action of light, an orientational ordering of the absorbing molecules takes place.

Several mechanisms of the photoorientation process are considered. Historically, the first to be proposed was the mechanism of light-stimulated Brownian rotational diffusion. This mechanism can be realised if the energy of the absorbed photon, or at least a part of it, dissipating in the medium, increases for some time the temperature of the medium near the particle that has absorbed the light and, thus, increases its rotational mobility. The photoorientation process proceeding by this mechanism is observed experimentally very rarely.

Another mechanism that causes the photoorientation process is the photostimulated pseudorotation of molecules. Several examples are known of photochemical rearrangement of the light-absorbing centre, which leads to the formation of the same centre in a different spatial orientation. Thus, in the course of the photochemical decomposition of cyclopentadienylidene ketene in a low-temperature matrix of carbon oxide, the corresponding carbene is formed, which can change its orientation in space either by rotation in the matrix cell or by the mechanism of hydrogen atom transfer from one carbon centre to another. 8.6. Kinetics of topochemical reactions

Up to now we have predominantly considered reactions in which the reactants were impurity molecules in solids, mainly glassy ones. These include, for example, phototransformations of dyes in polymers, reactions in frozen solutions and others. As has already been noted, the peculiarities of the kinetics of these reactions are connected with the small translational and rotational mobility of the molecules, as well as with the structural non-equilibrium and inhomogeneity of the reaction centres. However, in the cases considered we still dealt with reactions of individual molecules, for which it was possible to operate with kinetic equations for concentrations (that is, the number of molecules per unit volume).

In this connection, there exist many other kinetic equations, which are derived either under other simplifying assumptions or are obtained using empirically introduced fitting parameters. The problem of constructing a quantitative theory of the kinetics of heterogeneous chemical reactions is complicated by the fact that the rate of these reactions depends on a large number of structural and dynamic factors (on the morphology of the reactant and product phases, the size and shape of the reacting particles, the diffusion coefficients, the presence of impurities and defects and the like). At the same time, kinetic data alone (degrees of conversion averaged over the sample) turn out to be insufficient for elucidating the regularities of the course of the reactions. To construct a theory of the rates of solid-state processes, kinetic measurements must be supplemented by a detailed structural characterisation of the materials in the course of the reactions.

In conclusion, the following conclusions can be drawn about chemical reactions in solids.

1. The kinetics of chemical reactions in solids is determined not only by the chemical nature of the proceeding reactions, but also by the structural peculiarities of the reaction medium.

2. A frequently observed peculiarity of reactions in solids is the existence of a distribution of the reacting particles over kinetic characteristics: rate constants and activation energies. This distribution is caused by the structural inhomogeneity of these materials at the microscopic (inhomogeneity of the molecular environment, orientational disorder of the reacting molecules) and macroscopic (polycrystallinity of the materials, inhomogeneity of light absorption in photochemical reactions) levels. In the case when the process of restoration of the equilibrium distributions of the molecules over kinetic parameters proceeds with characteristic times greater than or comparable to the times of chemical reactions, the evolution of such distributions must be considered explicitly.

3. Chemical reactions in solids are often accompanied by phase transformations. This leads to the localisation of the chemical process near the interphase boundaries and to the dependence of the reaction kinetics on the geometric characteristics of the reaction space. In constructing kinetic models of such reactions, it is necessary to take explicitly into account the processes of nucleation and growth of the new phase.

On the whole, the kinetics of chemical transformations proceeding in solids is the least studied part of chemical kinetics, and one may hope that in the future new interesting results will be obtained in this direction and important generalisations will be made.
